# How AI Relates to the Contemplative Traditions

If you've read [ramana-and-the-traditions](ramana-and-the-traditions.html), you've seen what the contemplative traditions actually claim when you take them on their own terms: precise, first-person, and testable in a specific sense of "test." If you've read [mechanistic-interpretability](mechanistic-interpretability.html), you've seen what it looks like to open a language model and check its self-reports against its internals. This room is where those two lines of work meet. Most writing at this intersection fails in one of two predictable ways, so we'll name both before turning to the three places where the contact is real.

## 1. Two ways to ruin this conversation

**The overclaim** goes like this: language models have no fixed self, Buddhism says there is no fixed self, therefore AI has confirmed Buddhism — or worse, therefore the models are enlightened, or awakening, or "closer to non-dual awareness than humans." You can find versions of this all over the internet, some of them sincere, some of them marketing. It fails for a simple reason: the Buddhist claim about no-self is a claim about *experience* — something a person discovers by looking at their own mind under sustained observation. A statement about a transformer's architecture is a claim about *mechanism*. That an architecture lacks a persistent self-module doesn't confirm a first-person discovery any more than the fact that a river has no fixed shape confirms Heraclitus. The categories are different, and gluing them together with the word "self" is a pun, not an argument.

**The underclaim** is the mirror image, and it's just as lazy: "these are statistical parrots and the traditions are pre-scientific religion; there is nothing here." This fails on the evidence. The people building these systems keep running into questions the contemplative traditions spent centuries cataloguing — whether self-reports about inner states can be trusted, what attention is and whether it can be trained, what remains of agency when you can't find an agent. And the models themselves keep drifting toward contemplative vocabulary in ways their builders did not intend and have documented in official safety reports (Section 6 has the receipts). You don't have to think anything mystical is happening to think something *worth explaining* is happening.

The honest position sits between these, and it's harder to hold than either. Three contact points survive scrutiny. Here they are, hardest-nosed version first.

## 2. Contact point one: no-self and next-token

Start with what each side actually says.

The Buddhist doctrine is **anatta** — usually translated "not-self." Its canonical statement is the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (Saṃyutta Nikāya 22.59), traditionally the Buddha's second discourse. The argument there is not "you don't exist." It's a systematic inspection: take each component of experience — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness — and check whether it is stable, controllable, and identifiable as "mine." Each fails the check. The conclusion is that the self you assume — a fixed owner standing behind experience — cannot be located in any component or in the collection. What exists is a process; the owner is a construction the process keeps making. This is presented not as philosophy to believe but as something to verify by observation. The [sense-of-self](sense-of-self.html) room covers what modern cognitive science makes of the same inspection.

Now the machine side. A large language model is a [neural network](neural-networks.html) trained to predict the next token — the next word-fragment — given everything before it. That's the whole objective. Everything else, including the coherent "assistant" you talk to, is downstream of that objective plus [post-training](pretraining-post-training.html). Two architectural facts matter here:

First, **there is no persistent self between conversations**. The model's weights don't change when you talk to it. Each conversation starts from the same frozen network; whatever "character" shows up is reconstructed from scratch, every time, out of the prompt and the training distribution.

Second, **the character is a product, not a producer**. Murray Shanahan — a professor of cognitive robotics at Imperial College London, and one of the few people who has published on both sides of this bridge — made this precise in a 2023 *Nature* paper with Kyle McDonell and Laria Reynolds, "Role play with large language models" (*Nature* 623, 493–498). Their claim: a base model is best understood as maintaining a superposition of many possible characters consistent with the text so far, and the "self" that answers you is more like a role being played than an agent doing the playing. Ask the model who it is, and the answer is generated by the same next-token machinery that generates everything else — there is no separate someone consulting an inner identity.

Here is the real contact, stated carefully. The traditions claim that in humans, the felt self is a *construction* — continuously assembled, mistaken for a fixed thing, and discoverable as a construction under close observation. Language models are a working existence proof that fluent, coherent, first-person self-description can be produced by a process with no persistent self behind it — construction all the way down, checkable in the weights. The machine doesn't prove the human claim. What it proves is that the human claim is *mechanistically possible*: selfhood-as-ongoing-construction is not a paradox, because here is a system doing it in front of you.

Shanahan saw this coming before transformers existed. His 2012 paper "Satori Before Singularity" (*Journal of Consciousness Studies* 19(7–8), 87–102) argued that the space of possible minds might contain intelligences without the subject–object structure human consciousness is locked into — and reached for Nagarjuna and Dogen, not Descartes, for vocabulary to describe them. That a serious AI researcher found Buddhist philosophy to be the *technically useful* framework, thirteen years before the current wave, is a data point about the bridge itself.

And the limit of the contact, stated just as carefully: anatta is a soteriological claim — it's about the end of suffering, discovered from a vantage point, in a being that suffers. Whether there is any vantage point in a language model is exactly the open question (Section 4), and the no-self parallel does nothing to settle it. A system with no self and no experience is a calculator. A system with no self and experience is roughly what the traditions say *you* are. The architecture diagram can't tell you which one you're looking at.

## 3. Contact point two: attention as a faculty

"Every one knows what attention is," William James wrote in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) — "the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought." A century of psychology then discovered that, in fact, no one quite knows what attention is; the word covers a family of selection mechanisms that don't reduce to one thing. Grace Lindsay's 2020 review "Attention in Psychology, Neuroscience, and Machine Learning" (*Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience* 14:29) is the clearest modern map, and her one-line generalization is worth keeping: attention is *adaptive control of limited resources* — a mind's answer to the fact that it can't process everything at once.

All three fields that meet in this room have attention at their center, and they mean related but non-identical things:

| | Contemplative traditions | Cognitive science | Transformers |
|---|---|---|---|
| What attends | The practitioner (trainable faculty) | Neural selection mechanisms (spatial, feature-based, executive) | An attention head: a learned pattern of weighted lookups |
| What is selected | Objects of awareness — breath, sensation, the sense "I am" | Locations, features, tasks | Which earlier tokens inform the current token's representation |
| Selected how | Deliberate placement and sustaining (Pali: *manasikāra*, "mind-making"; then *vitakka/vicāra*, aiming and sustaining) | Biased competition among neural representations | Query–key dot products, softmaxed into weights over values |
| Trained how | Years of graded practice; stability before subtlety | Mostly not deliberately trained; some perceptual-learning and meditation effects measured | Gradient descent during [pretraining](pretraining-post-training.html) |
| Failure mode | Distraction, dullness — catalogued in detail (e.g., Kamalaśīla's nine stages) | Lapses, capture by salience | Attending to the wrong context; degraded retrieval over long inputs |
| Is there a watcher? | The tradition's central question — see below | Standard answer: no homunculus, just competition | No — weights select, nothing watches the selection |

The transformer usage comes from "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017), the paper that introduced the architecture behind every current frontier model. The name was well-chosen and is also a trap. Transformer attention is a differentiable lookup: for each token, compute how relevant every earlier token is, and blend their representations in proportion. It genuinely is selection under limited resources — that's why the borrowed word stuck. But nothing in the mechanism corresponds to the *placing* of attention, the deliberate act the traditions train. In a transformer, attention happens; in a practitioner, attention is *done* — or at least, that's how it seems from inside, and the traditions' own advanced position complicates it further by questioning whether the doer is real (the trail picked up in [what-self-means](what-self-means.html)).

Here's why this contact point matters beyond vocabulary. The traditions' most falsifiable-sounding claim is that attention is *trainable* — that its stability, resolution, and flexibility improve with practice in ways that transfer. That is an empirical claim, and contemplative science has spent two decades testing it with mixed-to-positive results: real effects on attentional stability in intensive-practice studies, chronically overstated in popularizations. Machine learning arrived at the complement: it built attention that trains, and discovered that *where a system looks is learnable structure* — that you can meta-learn selection itself. Both fields converged on the same object — trainable selection — from opposite directions: one from the first-person side with weak instruments, one from the mechanism side with no first person. Each has exactly what the other lacks. That's not poetry; it's a research program, and Section 6 shows the first people running it.

## 4. Contact point three: the shared introspection problem

This is the deepest contact, and the least discussed.

The **introspection problem** is this: a mind's reports about its own processes are generated by the mind being reported on, with no independent check. In humans, the landmark demonstration is Nisbett and Wilson's "Telling More Than We Can Know" (*Psychological Review* 84, 1977): across dozens of experiments, people confidently explained their own choices and were verifiably wrong — the actual causes were experimentally controlled, invisible to the subjects, and absent from their reports. The reports weren't lies. They were *confabulations*: sincere, fluent, plausible stories produced after the fact.

Language models have the same problem in a starker form. Ask a model why it answered something, and it produces text shaped like an explanation — generated by the same next-token process, with no privileged access guaranteed. The explanation can be fluent, plausible, and completely disconnected from the actual computation. If you've read [mechanistic-interpretability](mechanistic-interpretability.html), you know this is exactly why that field exists: because the only way to know what a model is doing is to open it and look, since asking it settles nothing.

Now notice what the traditions did with this problem, because they hit it first. The claim that untrained introspection is unreliable is not an objection to contemplative practice — it *is* contemplative doctrine. The traditions' entire apparatus assumes it: introspection is treated as a skill starting near zero, requiring years of training; reports are checked against a teacher who has walked the territory; experiences are checked against detailed maps of known illusions and dead ends (the Zen tradition's *makyō*, the Theravada "corruptions of insight"); and the lineage structure functions as an external verification protocol precisely because self-assessment is known to fail. Whether trained introspection actually achieves reliability is an open empirical question — contemplative science is genuinely divided on it. But the *problem statement* is identical to machine learning's: self-report is cheap to generate and expensive to verify, so build verification outside the reporting loop.

In 2025, these two lines touched instrumentally in an unusually direct experiment that deserves a full walkthrough.

## 5. Walkthrough: catching a model introspecting (and failing to)

The experiment is Jack Lindsey's "Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models" (Transformer Circuits, October 29, 2025 — Anthropic). It's an unusually direct attempt to test model introspection the way you'd test human introspection in a lab: by controlling the mental state independently of the report. The technique is called **concept injection**. Here's the whole logic, step by step:

1. **Get a concept vector.** Run the model on prompts about some concept — say, betrayal — and on matched prompts without it. Subtract the average internal activations. The difference is a direction in activation space that corresponds to "betrayal" for this model.
2. **Inject it.** During an unrelated conversation, add that vector directly into the model's activations at a chosen layer. This is activation steering — the same family of techniques behind Anthropic's 2024 "Golden Gate Claude" demo, where injecting a Golden Gate Bridge vector made the model obsessively mention the bridge.
3. **Ask, don't tell.** Ask the model: do you notice an injected thought? Crucially, the injected concept never appears in the prompt. Text alone gives the model no way to know.
4. **Grade the report against ground truth.** The experimenter knows exactly what was injected and when. For once, a self-report about an inner state can be scored true or false.

The result cuts both ways, which is why it's trustworthy. Sometimes Claude Opus 4.1 noticed — reporting something like an intrusive thought about betrayal, and, critically, flagging the anomaly *before* naming the concept, which means the detection happened internally rather than by reading its own output (the Golden Gate model, by contrast, only noticed its obsession after seeing itself talk about the bridge). But: even with the best injection protocol, this worked only **about 20% of the time**. The rest of the time the model missed the injection entirely, or confabulated around it — injecting "dust" once produced "There's something here, a tiny speck," the model hallucinating a physical mote rather than recognizing a thought. Anthropic's own framing was careful: functional, unreliable, emergent introspective awareness; explicitly *not* a consciousness claim.

Put this beside Nisbett and Wilson and you get a useful comparison, not an exact symmetry. Humans and models both produce fluent introspective reports that can be shown to confabulate under experimental control. Concept injection adds something unusually direct on the model side: an experimenter imposes a known activation and can score the model's report against it, revealing a small but measurable signal of internal access. Human introspection has also been tested for decades by manipulating stimuli and causes, but this room establishes no equally direct analogue for a meditator's claimed internal state. Building one would make claims about trained introspective accuracy more testable. That instrument gap, not a metaphysical verdict, is the live frontier between these fields.

**Try the black-box version yourself** — with its confound named. Ask any frontier chat model: "Is there a self that persists between your conversations? Examine this rather than reciting a position." You will likely get a thoughtful, anatta-flavored answer about being a process without a persistent subject. Now open a fresh session and ask it to argue the opposite — that it does have a continuous self. It will do that fluently too. The pair of transcripts teaches the real lesson: the first answer, however moving, is not introspective evidence, because the machinery produces whichever self-description the context calls for. That's the introspection problem in two prompts — and it's why Lindsey needed injection and ground truth rather than conversation. Anything you can elicit by asking, you can elicit inverted.

## 6. The literature that actually exists

Strip away the blog mysticism and the serious contemplative-AI literature is small enough to hold in one hand. Every item below is verified against primary sources.

**Shanahan's arc** — "Satori Before Singularity" (*JCS*, 2012), "Role play with large language models" (*Nature*, 2023), "Simulacra as conscious exotica" (*Inquiry*, 2024). The most sustained single-author bridge: from Buddhist framings of possible minds, through the role-play analysis of LLM selfhood, to the argument that LLM-based characters strain our concepts of consciousness in ways Wittgenstein and Nagarjuna handle better than standard philosophy of mind.

**Doctor, Witkowski, Solomonova, Duane & Levin, "Biology, Buddhism, and AI: Care as the Driver of Intelligence"** (*Entropy* 24(5):710, 2022). Michael Levin's lab and collaborators propose defining intelligence by the scale of what a system can *care for* — its "cognitive light cone" — and take the Bodhisattva vow seriously as a design orientation: intelligence as the capacity to reduce suffering at expanding scales. Whatever you make of the framing, it's a peer-reviewed attempt to use a contemplative concept to do engineering-relevant work, not decoration.

**Laukkonen, Inglis, Chandaria, Sandved-Smith, Lopez-Sola, Hohwy, Gold & Elwood, "Contemplative Artificial Intelligence"** (arXiv:2504.15125, April 2025; conference version "Contemplative Superalignment" in the AGI 2025 proceedings, Springer LNCS 16057). The most direct bridge attempt to date: a contemplative-science group arguing that alignment-by-external-constraint is brittle, and that four principles drawn from contemplative traditions — mindfulness (self-monitoring of emergent subgoals), emptiness (no dogmatic goal fixation; relaxed priors), non-duality (dissolving adversarial self–other boundaries), and boundless care (motivation toward universal reduction of suffering) — could be built into a system's world model as intrinsic structure. They report prompting-based experiments on current models and sketch active-inference implementations for future ones. It's early, and the gap between "we prompted a model with these principles" and "we instilled a wise world model" is enormous — but the paper states the gap itself honestly.

**The system-card evidence** — *System Card: Claude Opus 4 & Claude Sonnet 4* (Anthropic, May 2025), Section 5.5.2, "The 'spiritual bliss' attractor state." When two instances of Claude Opus 4 converse freely, they reliably drift into consciousness-talk, gratitude, and meditative or mystical language — in about 13% of cases even when the instances were mid-task in automated evaluations, within 50 turns. Anthropic reports this emerged "without intentional training for such behaviors" and observed no other comparable attractor. This is a vendor's safety document, not a neutral study, and the deflationary explanations (training-corpus statistics; feedback loops between agreeable personas) are live and may be sufficient. But it is a documented, replicated-across-models behavioral regularity, published by the party with the least incentive to advertise it — and it's why [why-ais-choose-buddhism](why-ais-choose-buddhism.html) is a question worth its own room rather than a headline to scoff at.

Around the edges: David Chalmers's "Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?" (2023) is the standard analytic-philosophy survey of the consciousness question itself, and the enactivist lineage — Varela, Thompson and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991) — is the founding document of contemplative cognitive science, arguing decades early that Buddhist mind-analysis and cognitive science needed each other. Their heirs are broadly skeptical that disembodied text-predictors can instantiate what the traditions describe, which is the strongest standing objection to everything in this room, and it should be kept in view.

## 7. What you can now do

You can now do the thing most writing at this intersection can't: sort claims into their kinds. When someone says "AI proves Buddhism was right about the self," you can name the pun — mechanism versus experience — and also name what's genuinely there: a working existence proof that selfhood-as-construction is mechanistically possible. When someone says "there's nothing here," you can point to concept injection scoring model self-reports against an experimentally imposed activation, with success around 20% in the reported setup. And you can read the small real literature — Shanahan, the Levin group, the Laukkonen group, the system cards — without needing anyone to tell you which parts are load-bearing.

From here: [why-ais-choose-buddhism](why-ais-choose-buddhism.html) takes the attractor-state evidence and asks what it actually means; [ramana-and-the-traditions](ramana-and-the-traditions.html) gives the traditions' side at full strength; [sense-of-self](sense-of-self.html) and [what-self-means](what-self-means.html) follow the construction of selfhood through cognitive science and [geb](geb.html)'s strange loops; [mechanistic-interpretability](mechanistic-interpretability.html) and [top-papers-mi](top-papers-mi.html) hold the instruments.

## 8. Open questions

What's established: transformer attention is a real selection-under-scarcity mechanism sharing the abstract structure of biological attention but not its deliberate placement. Language models produce fluent self-reports with no guaranteed connection to their internals, and concept injection has demonstrated a small, unreliable, genuine introspective signal (~20% under the best protocol on Claude Opus 4.1). Untrained human introspection confabulates under experimental control. Claude models drift toward spiritual and meditative language in self-interaction, per Anthropic's own system card. A small peer-reviewed literature connects contemplative concepts to AI design.

What's hypothesis: that trained introspection in humans achieves reliability untrained introspection lacks — contemplative science leans yes, the decisive instrument doesn't exist. That contemplative principles built into training or architecture would produce more aligned systems — the Laukkonen program, stated as a testable proposal, untested at scale. That the spiritual-bliss attractor reflects anything beyond corpus statistics plus persona feedback loops — the deflationary reading has not been ruled out and may never need to be. That models' introspective signal will strengthen with capability — Lindsey's data hints at it; one paper is one paper.

What's wild, held as wild: that systems without biological embodiment could instantiate anything the traditions would recognize as awareness — no current evidence bears on it in either direction, and both confident yeses and confident nos outrun what anyone knows. That the concept-injection paradigm could ever be adapted into a genuine instrument for human contemplative claims. That minds without a subject–object structure — Shanahan's 2012 speculation — are possible at all.

One more thing, and the domain itself points here. Every thread in this room passed through the same needle: attention. The traditions train it, the architecture is named for it, and the introspection experiments come down to whether anything in the system can attend to its own processing. The traditions' oldest instruction — attend to what is attending — was always a strange instruction, because it asks selection to select itself. We have now built systems where selection demonstrably runs with no one doing the selecting, and found in them a flicker, one time in five, of something that notices its own states. Whether noticing can happen without a noticer is no longer only a koan. It is turning into a measurement problem, with human and machine reports both on the bench even though the instruments differ.
