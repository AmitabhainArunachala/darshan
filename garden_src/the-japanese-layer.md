# The Japanese Layer of the AI Stack

If you've read [The Chip Wars](chip-wars.html), you already have the three-foundry, one-EUV-machine map. This room sits one layer down. The leading-edge wafer is still made in Taiwan, Korea, or Arizona; a surprising fraction of what that wafer is *made of*, and of the tools that cut and inspect it, is still made in Japan. Seeing the Silicon starts here because technological importance is not the same as economic capture, and economic capture is not the same as business quality, and business quality is not the same as investment attractiveness. Japan is the place where those four come apart most cleanly.

## 1. The map under the foundry

Start with what the Japanese equipment industry itself reports, not with a magazine ranking.

The Semiconductor Equipment Association of Japan (SEAJ) is the trade body for Japanese-made tools. In its July 2, 2026 forecast, SEAJ said sales of semiconductor manufacturing equipment made in Japan would reach **¥6.55 trillion in fiscal 2026** (April 2026–March 2027), up 26% from the prior year, on advanced-logic spend for AI servers and a sharp rise in DRAM, especially high-bandwidth memory. It then forecasts ¥7.40 trillion in fiscal 2027 and ¥7.77 trillion in fiscal 2028. Those are Japanese-made tools sold worldwide, not tools sold into Japan. The Japanese *market* is much smaller: SEAJ's January 2026 forecast put Japanese-market semiconductor-equipment sales at ¥1.58 trillion for fiscal 2026. Japan is a supplier geography, not a consumption geography.

SEMI's mid-year OEM forecast, published July 14, 2026, put the *global* semiconductor-equipment market at **$165.9 billion in 2026**. Electronics Weekly, on September 2, 2026, published a Q2 2026 vendor-share sketch for that market: Applied Materials 17–20%, ASML 16–19%, Lam Research 14–16%, **Tokyo Electron 11–14%**, KLA 6–8%, then a tail that includes SCREEN and Advantest at 2–3% each. TEL is a top-four equipment company by total sales. It is not a niche. What is Japanese about the Japanese layer is not the total-market ranking. It is the *step* ranking: a handful of process steps where one Japanese firm is first or second, and sometimes the only firm a leading-edge fab can actually buy from.

Tokyo Electron's own integrated report (the 2025 English chapter on the production-equipment business) states a **100% share, as TEL's estimate, of coater/developers for EUV lithography**. A coater/developer is the machine that spins photoresist — the light-sensitive film the circuit pattern is printed into — onto the wafer, and then develops it after exposure. TEL's April 30, 2026 earnings materials put its overall coater/developer share at **more than 90%**, and its dielectric-etch share at more than 50%. Those are company estimates, disclosed as such. Treat them as the company talking about itself, then look for a second instrument. The second instrument here is the process: every EUV scanner ASML ships needs a coater/developer in front of it. If TEL is the only qualified EUV coater, TEL's unit volume is a shadow of ASML's EUV shipments.

The rest of the equipment map, as of 2025–2026 filings and dated secondary surveys, looks like this. Where a company does not publish a share in the documents I opened, the table says so.

## 2. The materials Japan kept

Equipment is the visible half. Materials are the quieter half, and they are more Japanese.

**Wafers.** A 300 mm silicon wafer is the disc the circuit is built on. Nikkei's 2025 materials-share survey, reported in July 2026, put **Shin-Etsu Chemical at 26.3%** of the silicon-wafer market and **SUMCO at 17.8%**, a combined 44.1%, up 2.8 points from 2024. That is lower than the "two Japanese firms hold more than half" line that still circulates in English. The discrepancy is method: area versus revenue, 300 mm versus all diameters, calendar versus fiscal. Five firms — Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, Taiwan's GlobalWafers, Germany's Siltronic, Korea's SK Siltron — still account for most of the market. The honest statement as of 2025 is: two Japanese firms are first and second on Nikkei's survey; together they are short of half; they are more concentrated in advanced 300 mm epitaxial wafers, which is where AI logic and HBM sit. SEMI's Silicon Manufacturers Group reported 2024 global wafer-area shipments of 12,266 million square inches, down 2.7% year on year, with a recovery beginning in the second half of 2024. SUMCO said in 2026 that the 300 mm wafer market grew about 15% year on year in Q1 2026. Wafer pricing is a [capital cycle](the-capital-cycle.html) of its own: long contracts, slow furnaces, a glut that takes years to clear.

**Photoresist.** Photoresist is the film the scanner writes into. Nikkei's 2025 survey put three Japanese firms — Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK), JSR, and Shin-Etsu — in the top three photoresist slots, with a combined **60.5%**, up 0.3 points from 2024. Fujifilm and Sumitomo Chemical are the other Japanese names in the EUV-resist field; DuPont and Korea's Dongjin Semichem are the usual non-Japanese comparables. Company-level EUV-resist shares are not published in the filings I opened. "Japanese firms, plural, still dominate advanced resist" is the claim that survives; a single-firm EUV-resist monopoly is not a claim I can source to a primary document.

**ABF.** Ajinomoto Build-up Film is the insulating film inside the package substrate of a high-end CPU or GPU — the layer that lets the substrate carry fine traces between the chip and the board. Ajinomoto Fine-Techno, a subsidiary of the food company, developed it from amino-acid chemistry. The ~95% high-end ABF-film share is widely reported (Palliser Capital's March 2026 campaign letter states "~95% global market share in ABF" and "~100% share in high-end AI data center applications"). **Ajinomoto does not publish that share in the filings I opened.** Treat 95% as a repeated secondary figure, not as a measured census. Sekisui Chemical is the named challenger, usually described as a small single-digit share. Qualification of a new film at a substrate maker is measured in years, which is why a food-company subsidiary can remain the constraint. That is [necessity and capture](necessity-and-capture.html) in one material.

**Substrates.** The film is not the board. Ibiden, Shinko Electric, and a set of Taiwanese firms (Unimicron, Nan Ya PCB) and Austria's AT&S make the ABF substrates themselves. Published shares disagree by definition: overall ABF-substrate revenue versus high-end GPU substrates versus layer-count mix. QYResearch-type compilations put Unimicron around 22% of ABF substrates and the top five around three-quarters; other 2026 notes put Ibiden higher, especially in high-end GPU work, and cite a ¥500 billion Ibiden capex plan across FY2026–2028. **Ibiden's own filings are the document; a single "Ibiden has X% of AI substrates" number is not one I will invent.** The structural fact is enough: Japan is present in the film and in one of the high-end substrate makers; Taiwan is present in the volume substrate names. [Advanced packaging](advanced-packaging.html) is the room that follows the substrate into CoWoS and hybrid bonding.

**The rest of the materials list, stated honestly.** Resonac (the old Hitachi Chemical plus Showa Denko) is widely described as a leader in CMP slurry — the liquid that, with Ebara's or Applied's polishers, flattens the wafer. Resonac's IR discusses a leading CMP-slurry position; a precise global percentage is not in the documents I opened. Namics makes underfills and encapsulants, the resins that fill the gap under a flip-chip; share not published. LINTEC makes back-grinding tape and dicing tape, the films that hold a wafer while DISCO thins and cuts it; share not published. Sumitomo Chemical makes resist and other process chemicals. These names belong on the map because a leading-edge flow uses them. They do not belong in a fake-precision share column.

## 3. Why Japan lost logic and kept this layer

In 1986, six of the world's top ten semiconductor companies by sales were Japanese. NEC, Hitachi, and Toshiba sat in the first three seats. Japan's strength was DRAM: the memory chip that holds a bit as charge in a capacitor, and that the personal-computer boom of the 1980s consumed in bulk. Takashi Yunogami (湯之上隆), who joined Hitachi's DRAM group in 1987 at that peak, has spent the years since writing the decline from the inside. His account is not "the Americans cheated." It is a process account: DRAM became a commodity, Korean firms (first Samsung) ran a steeper yield-and-cost curve, Japanese conglomerates treated the semiconductor division as one division among many, and the move from memory to logic — from a standardized part to a designed part — was a move Japan did not make in time.

The 1986 US–Japan Semiconductor Arrangement is the political date everyone cites. Japan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs archives record the path: the US Semiconductor Industry Association filed a Section 301 case in June 1985; dumping cases followed; on July 30, 1986 the two governments agreed on improved foreign access to the Japanese market, suspension of US dumping investigations, and Japanese monitoring of export prices, including in third-country markets; the letters were exchanged on **September 2, 1986**. A side letter, not in the public text of the arrangement, is widely reported to have aimed at a 20% foreign share of the Japanese market. Reagan's administration added 100% tariffs on selected Japanese goods in April 1987 when it judged compliance slow. Brookings, restating METI's own later numbers, puts Japan's global semiconductor *production* share at nearly 50% in the late 1980s and around 10% in recent years.

The arrangement mattered. It is not the whole cause. Tsugio Makimoto (牧本次雄), who ran Hitachi's semiconductor group through that era, named a longer cycle: **Makimoto's wave**, the roughly ten-year oscillation between standardized products (DRAM, microprocessors-as-parts) and customized products (ASICs, then SoCs, then the AI accelerator as a designed object). Japan won the standardized-memory half of a wave. It did not win the customized-logic half that followed. Korea took DRAM. Taiwan, through TSMC's foundry model, took logic. The United States kept design. Japan kept the steps that a foundry still has to buy from someone: wafers, resist, coaters, cleaners, dicing saws, certain slurries, certain films.

That split is the Japanese layer. It is also why a 2019 export-control dispute over three Japanese chemicals could rattle Samsung within weeks, and why a Rapidus fab in Hokkaido is a bet on reversing one half of the split without giving up the other. The 1980s fight was about televisions and memory for spreadsheets. This one includes where the accelerators in [nvidia-and-the-chip](nvidia-and-the-chip.html) get made, and under whose materials. [Governments and AI](governments-and-ai.html) and [the China–USA race](china-usa-race.html) are the altitude at which that fight is named; this room is the bill of materials.

## 4. Rapidus at Chitose: what would have to be true by 2027

Rapidus is a foundry, founded August 10, 2022, trying to skip from Japan's domestic logic (decades behind the leading edge) to a 2-nanometer-class gate-all-around process developed with IBM. The factory is **IIM-1** — Innovative Integration for Manufacturing — in Chitose, Hokkaido. The Japanese government has treated it as a national project. METI's implementation-plan summary states the operational target: **start 2 nm-class mass production in the second half of fiscal 2027**, and aim for a listing around fiscal 2031.

What has actually happened, dated, from Rapidus's own notices:

- November 2022: NEDO adopted the US–Japan 2 nm integration and short-TAT manufacturing project.
- December 2024: Rapidus began installing Japan's first production EUV tool at IIM-1.
- April 2025: the IIM-1 pilot line started up. Rapidus later reported 2 nm GAA transistor operation on 300 mm wafers as a 2025-year target that was met.
- A separate chiplet-packaging project, Rapidus Chiplet Solutions, was adopted in March 2024 and built a back-end pilot at Epson's Chitose site, including a 600 mm-square organic RDL interposer prototype that Rapidus called an industry first.
- April 11, 2026: NEDO approved the FY2026 plan and budget for both projects. Rapidus said it would release the PDK customers need to design, put the short-TAT production system onto the pilot line, and pursue a defect-density target within the fiscal year. CEO Atsuyoshi Koike's statement tied that work to "mass production start targeted for 2027."
- As of February 27, 2026, Rapidus listed capital (including capital reserve) of ¥274.95 billion.

A pilot line with working transistors is not a business. The questions that decide whether IIM-1 is a success by the end of 2027 are specific, and they are the questions [forecasting](forecasting.html) would force you to write down before you attach a probability.

**What would have to be true by the end of fiscal 2027 for IIM-1 to be judged a success, stated so a later reader can score it:**

1. **A production PDK in customers' hands.** Rapidus told Nikkei in 2026 that PDK 1.0 — the kit a customer needs to tape out a production chip, not a test chip — was planned for late 2026 or early 2027, with a shuttle of customer test chips around the same window. If PDK 1.0 has not been released to at least one external customer by March 31, 2028, the 2027 mass-production date has slipped in the only way that matters.
2. **An external customer of record, not a demonstration.** Mass production means a named customer's die is being built for a product, at a yield the customer accepted. A working IBM-derived transistor in a Rapidus press notice does not count. The resolving source is a customer 10-K, a Rapidus shipment disclosure, or a teardown of a shipping part.
3. **Yield that is a manufacturing number, not a lab number.** Defect density on the pilot line is the FY2026 NEDO target Rapidus itself named. A 2027 success is a yield on a customer product, disclosed or inferable, in the same conversation as TSMC N2 or Intel 18A — not "transistors met planned electricals."
4. **A funding path that is not only the state.** METI's plan sketches several trillion yen of combined R&D and production investment, with government outlays already in the multiple-trillions-of-yen range across NEDO and related vehicles. A foundry that reaches 2027 still funded almost entirely by the taxpayer has not yet answered the business-quality question, even if the transistors switch.

History is against national-champion leading-edge logic. Japan's materials and equipment position, the IBM process, and the fact of a running EUV pilot line are real assets. Both sides of that sentence can be true. Rapidus is the test of whether the layer Japan kept can be used to buy back the layer it lost.

## 5. Worked example: reconstructing DISCO from its own filings

Here is the method this room is for. You can run it on TEL, SCREEN, Lasertec, Shin-Etsu, or Ibiden. DISCO is the cleanest teaching case because the company named the business in three verbs and then published the numbers that tell you what those verbs are worth.

**Step 1 — what it sells, in its own words.** DISCO's business theme is **Kiru, Kezuru, Migaku**: cut, grind, polish. In semiconductor language: a **dicing saw** separates a finished wafer into dies; a **grinder** thins the wafer from the back so the package can be short; a **polisher** finishes that surface. The company also sells the blades, wheels, and related **precision processing tools** — consumables — that wear out as the equipment runs. President Kazuma Sekiya's June 2026 investor message puts the FY2025 (April 2025–March 2026) year in one paragraph: generative-AI and data-center demand kept high-performance semiconductor equipment strong; PCs, smartphones, and power devices did not fully recover; equipment shipments were strong in high-value-added tools; consumable shipments were strong in line with customers' utilization. Net sales and profits hit records; profit was a record for a sixth consecutive year.

**Step 2 — the numbers from the 有価証券報告書, not from a blog.** DISCO's 87th-period annual securities report, filed June 16, 2026, for the year ended March 31, 2026:

| | FY2024 (ended Mar 2025) | FY2025 (ended Mar 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Net sales | ¥393.313 billion | **¥436.889 billion** |
| Ordinary profit | ¥168.943 billion | **¥184.936 billion** |
| Ordinary margin | 43.0% | **42.3%** |
| Net income attributable to owners | ¥123.891 billion | ¥135.521 billion |
| Equity ratio | 75.1% | 78.9% |
| ROE | 27.6% | 25.1% |
| Operating cash flow | ¥120.364 billion | ¥133.543 billion |
| Investing cash flow | −¥68.002 billion | −¥135.769 billion |

Q1 of FY2026 (April–June 2026), from DISCO's own earnings summary: net sales ¥114.3 billion, up 27.1% year on year; operating margin **42.9%**; gross margin **71.4%**; overseas sales **90.8%** of the quarter.

**Step 3 — what the margins say about position.** A 42% operating margin and a 70%+ gross margin, sustained across years, is not the signature of a commodity tool vendor competing on price against three peers. It is the signature of a process step where the customer cannot easily dual-source, where the installed base pulls consumables, and where the next node (thinner wafers, stealth dicing, hybrid-bonding prep) keeps moving the qualification bar. DISCO does **not** publish a global dicing or grinding share in this filing. Third-party estimates in 2025–2026 commonly sit in a 60–80% band for dicing saws and around 70% for grinders. Use those as leads. The filing is the source for the margin, the mix (equipment plus consumables), the overseas concentration, and the fact that AI-related high-performance semiconductors were the demand that printed the record.

**Step 4 — who the customer is, without pretending DISCO named them.** DISCO's filing discusses application mix (generative AI and data-center semiconductors strong; automotive and power sluggish) and does not, in the pages I opened, give a customer-concentration table with names. The 90.8% overseas share in Q1 FY2026 tells you the customer is not "Japan's remaining IDMs." It is the foundries and OSATs — Taiwan, Korea, and the rest of the manufacturing map in [taiwan](taiwan.html) and [semiconductors](semiconductors.html). The dicing and thinning step sits after the front-end and next to [advanced packaging](advanced-packaging.html): HBM stacks and chiplet assemblies are grind-and-cut problems as much as they are lithography problems. DISCO's own December 2025 tech briefing treated hybrid bonding as a grinding-and-polishing story. That is how a back-end toolmaker ends up on the AI bill of materials.

**Step 5 — the pattern for the rest of the list.** For any Japanese name in the table above: (1) open the latest 有価証券報告書 or 決算短信, (2) write down sales, operating or ordinary margin, overseas ratio, and any product-mix split the company actually prints, (3) write "share not published" unless the company printed a share, (4) only then allow a Gartner or Nikkei share as a dated secondary, labeled as such. TEL's 100% EUV-coater claim survives this test because TEL printed it as its own estimate. Ajinomoto's 95% ABF figure does not, yet, survive as a company-printed number in the documents I opened. The difference is the whole method of this wing.

## 6. What you can now see

You can now look at an English-language AI-supply-chain article and notice the hole. It will name TSMC, ASML, Nvidia, maybe Samsung. It will rarely name the coater in front of the EUV scanner, the wafer the scanner prints, the resist on that wafer, the film inside the substrate, or the saw that singulates the die. Those are the Japanese layer. You can also see why Japan lost logic without losing the steps logic still has to buy: DRAM peaked, the 1986 arrangement and the Korean cost curve did their work, Makimoto's customized-product wave went to the foundry model, and the materials-and-equipment firms stayed on a different cycle — slower, more qualified, harder to dual-source.

From here: [necessity-and-capture](necessity-and-capture.html) is the wing's law applied to quartz, resist, and EUV. [The capital cycle](the-capital-cycle.html) is what happens when a step like ABF substrates or wafer-fab equipment goes through shortage and glut. [What are those rings](what-are-those-rings.html) is one consumable, one causal chain. [Bottleneck-migration](bottleneck-migration.html) is what happens when the constraint moves off this layer. [Power and cooling](power-and-cooling.html) is the layer the accelerator hits after it leaves the package. [Physical accounting](physical-accounting.html) counts the energy and materials of one accelerator with the boundaries stated.

## Open questions

**Established (FACT):** SEAJ's July 2026 forecast for Japanese-made semiconductor equipment (¥6.55 trillion in FY2026). TEL's own April 2026 statements on EUV coater/developer share and dielectric-etch share. DISCO's FY2025 filing: ¥436.889 billion sales, 42.3% ordinary margin, sixth consecutive record profit. Nikkei 2025 materials survey: Shin-Etsu 26.3% and SUMCO 17.8% of silicon wafers; TOK/JSR/Shin-Etsu combined 60.5% of photoresist. Rapidus IIM-1 pilot line in operation from April 2025; NEDO FY2026 plan approved April 11, 2026; mass-production target still stated as 2027. The 1986 arrangement's public terms and September 2, 1986 letter-exchange, per MOFA's archival summary.

**Contested (HYPOTHESIS):** Whether Rapidus can convert a working pilot line into customer-bearing 2 nm production by the second half of fiscal 2027. Whether Ajinomoto's ABF position is as close to a monopoly as secondary sources claim — the company does not print the 95% figure in the filings I opened. Whether Japanese wafer share is "about half" or "about 44%," which is a method fight, not a small rounding. Whether Lasertec's actinic-EUV position remains a practical monopoly once High-NA volumes rise and KLA's non-actinic tools take more of the inspection budget.

**Speculation worth holding (WILD):** That the next chip war is fought less over logic fabs and more over the qualified materials list — resist, ABF, high-end substrates, actinic inspection — because those lists change slower than node names. That a successful Rapidus, if it happens, still leaves Japan a materials-and-equipment power that happens to own one small leading-edge foundry, rather than a restored 1988.

Signposts, dated and falsifiable: (1) If Rapidus has not released PDK 1.0 to an external customer by March 31, 2028, the FY2027 mass-production target has failed on Rapidus's own stated path; resolving source: Rapidus notice or a customer's process-design disclosure. (2) If Nikkei's next annual materials-share survey (expected mid-2027, covering 2026) shows Shin-Etsu plus SUMCO below 40% of silicon wafers, the "Japan kept wafers" half of this room has started to move; resolving source: that survey. (3) If TEL, in an FY2027 earnings presentation, reports an EUV coater/developer share materially under 90% as its own estimate, the coater chokepoint has a second source; resolving source: TEL IR.

---

One more thing, and it comes from the layer itself. The Japanese companies in this room do not make the model. They make the disc, the film, the spin, the cut. A language model is a stack of matrix multiplies sitting on [the memory wall](the-memory-wall.html); those multiplies only exist because a wafer was grown, coated, exposed, cleaned, thinned, and diced by firms most English-language coverage never names. What a supply chain pays attention to is what it can govern. Japan lost the object everyone watches and kept the objects almost nobody watches. The discipline of this wing is to watch those anyway — not because they are more important than the foundry, but because they are the part of the machine that does not show up in a keynote.
