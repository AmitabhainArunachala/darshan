---

There is a table with nine hundred million cells, and inside it, by the paper's definition, is a society.

The table has four dimensions: which of ten thousand demographic profiles is speaking, which of ten thousand is listening, and which of three stances — agree, neutral, disagree — each currently holds. Look up any combination and the table tells you how the listener comes out of the conversation. That is the whole exchange. In the largest social simulation ever run, one billion agents formed and changed their opinions this way — one hundred rounds, some two million influencers speaking in each — and every meeting between two of those billion minds was, in the paper's own words, "a single array lookup."

The paper is called "Modeling Earth-Scale Human-Like Societies with One Billion Agents." It was first posted in June 2025 by ten authors, thirteen in the 2026 revision, centered on Zhongguancun Academy. Their framework is Light Society. The billion-agent result is real, in the sense that the computation ran. What the billion *is* — that is the reading this column exists to do.

---

Start by honoring what is actually there, because it is considerable.

Three years ago the state of this art was a video-game village: twenty-five language-model agents in a sandbox town called Smallville, waking up, cooking breakfast, spreading invitations to a Valentine's party that began as one idea planted in one agent's mind — the other twenty-four heard of it, asked each other on dates, and showed up, none of it scripted. Since then the counts have climbed — a thousand, ten thousand, a million — and the engineering that gets you from a million to a billion is genuinely clever. You cannot run a billion language models. So the Light Society team taught a small, cheap network to imitate a large one: for each topic, they had a full LLM play out roughly four hundred thousand of these opinion exchanges, distilled its behavior into a lightweight model, then precomputed that model's answer for every possible combination of who-meets-whom. The society runs on the residue of a language model's judgment, frozen into a table.

And the team is careful in ways the viral coverage was not. They ground their agents in profiles drawn from the World Values Survey, an academic instrument that has interviewed people in dozens of countries for decades. They report that in the Ultimatum Game their agents' mean offer lands "squarely within the 40%–50% range commonly reported in human experiments," where a purely rational agent would offer almost nothing — the agents reproduce a familiar human preference for fair offers. They warn, in the revised paper, that any single model's simulation should be treated as "one realization in a family of LLM-mediated social dynamics," never "a neutral oracle." The methods are explicit; the question is what this engineering result establishes about people.

---

Now trace the number, because the number is the headline, and this house traces headlines.

One billion agents. Follow the chain down: the billion share ten thousand distinct profiles among them — each "person" in this earth-scale society is one of ten thousand borrowed faces, photocopied a hundred thousand times. The profiles come from a cleaned pool of 96,125 survey respondents. The agents' decisions come not from a mind per agent but from one distilled network — an imitation of one language model, Gemini 2.0 Flash, prompted to role-play those profiles — and at the full billion even that is too slow, so it is replaced by the precomputed table. An agent, at earth scale, is a row index.

Is that a scandal? No — it is roughly how all large simulation works, and the authors describe every step in the open. But it matters for what the title invites you to believe. "Earth-scale human-like societies" suggests the planet got a digital twin. What was demonstrated is that opinion propagation, on one synthetic network shape, among copies of ten thousand personas, with three permitted stances, can be computed at planetary count — and that the curves stay smooth when you swap the expensive model for the cheap one. The field's own literature is blunt about what stands between that and the title. Language models flatten the people they impersonate: their survey answers resemble Western, educated, industrialized populations and degrade as you move away from them; they show less variance than real humans show; in one study, minor changes to the persona format and the framing of the game instructions shifted simulated cooperation rates by up to seventy-six percentage points. One political-science team found that even where simulated averages looked remarkably close to the real survey's, nearly half the estimated relationships between variables differed significantly from the human data — and among those, the sign flipped a third of the time. Coarse resemblance, divergent relationships — a mirage that photographs well. And a 2026 result cuts at the billion itself: another team showed a ten-million-agent opinion simulation could be approximately reproduced with 381 times fewer model calls, the final opinion distribution diverging only slightly (JSD 0.094) from the full run's — which is to say, most of those agents were not adding information. The billion establishes throughput, not validity: the run proves the system scales, not that its agents preserve human variation or relationships.

Our reading of the result—not the authors' conclusion, though their caveats walk most of the way—is narrower: nobody has simulated an earth-scale human-like society. Somebody has built a machine that could rehearse one narrow kind of social weather at planetary count, and shown the machine runs.

---

The question this house exists to ask is not whether the paper is good. It is: *what is being practiced here, and on whom?*

The demonstration domain the team chose — the one thing their billion-agent society actually does — is opinion diffusion. Two hundred million influencer nodes, eight hundred million receivers, seeded stances propagating through the crowd. They chose it because it is a canonical problem with clean mathematics. But notice what the instrument is, functionally: a rehearsal space for the question *if we say this to everyone, what will everyone come to believe?*

You do not have to take a magazine's word that this matters. Take the researchers'. The revised paper says: "A planet-scale, controllable simulator of opinion diffusion lowers the barrier not only to legitimate research but also, in principle, to the design of large-scale information operations and persuasive content; we therefore intend the released framework as an instrument for studying such dynamics, not for executing them." That sentence deserves respect — it names the danger more plainly than most coverage did. It also deserves a date: it appears in the June 2026 revision. The original version, posted a year earlier, contained no ethics discussion at all. The capability shipped first; the warning arrived with the second draft.

And the surrounding world is already moving. In a preregistered trial published in Nature Human Behaviour, GPT-4 given a debate partner's basic personal information raised its odds of moving that person's view by 81.2 percent relative to human persuaders — personalization, the exact conditioning population simulators are built from, is the active ingredient. In 2025, University of Zurich researchers were caught running undisclosed AI personas on Reddit for months, tailoring arguments to each target's inferred "gender, age, ethnicity, location, and political orientation." A startup called Aaru sells simulated audiences — synthetic crowds standing in for surveys and focus groups — to businesses and political campaigns; Accenture invested. The synthetic crowd renders its verdict before any real person is asked.

The regulatory gap is concrete: the EU and Chinese provisions examined here govern manipulation of people and labeling of content, not the synthetic cohort used before deployment; downstream uses may still be regulated. The EU's AI Act prohibits manipulative systems deployed *against* real people and requires synthetic *content* to be labeled. China's provisions govern recommendation algorithms aimed *at* the public and synthetic media — its generative-AI rules apply only to services offered to the public, leaving research use explicitly outside them.

The epistemics run deeper than the law. An anthropologist and a psychologist warned in Nature in 2024 that AI "surrogates" for human participants invite a science where "we produce more but understand less" — the simulation consulted because it is faster and cheaper than people. One step past their page: the consulted simulation slowly becomes the thing policymakers mean by "the public." Machine-learning theory has a name for what happens next: performative prediction — "When predictions support decisions they may influence the outcome they aim to predict." The theory was built for credit scores and traffic forecasts, but the loop is the same. A government that road-tests policy on a simulated population, a platform that tunes its feed against simulated users, is not measuring the world; it is steering by a model whose steering changes the world.

---

We brought Shantideva into this reading because his universal address resembles the paper's planetary scale. The resemblance may be only grammatical.

His *Bodhicharyavatara* — "the way of the bodhisattva," eighth century — confronts its own version of the question: how does one mind hold a billion minds? His practice of equalizing self and other begins there: "All have the same sorrows, the same joys as I, and I must guard them like myself." And the argument is strangely bare: my pain moves me simply because it is pain; yours is also pain; therefore "I must destroy the pain of another as though it were my own, because it is a pain." His ninth chapter divides all truth in two — "We deem that there are two verities, the Veiled Truth and the Transcendent Reality" — and every model, including the mind's own, sits on the veiled side. The oldest mistake there is, is forgetting which one you are holding.

But their shared cardinality conceals different premises: one assumes enough behavioral regularity for prediction; the other argues that ownership should not determine whether suffering matters. Moral equality is not empirical substitutability. And the vow must stand under the same fire as the table: a vow to serve "all beings" can repeat the model's error — the universal frame can erase the person who refuses its description, and service becomes paternalism when the servant decides what another needs. Light Society publishes its reductions — ten thousand profiles, three stances, a table. Compassion has no equivalent audit. Shantideva supplies an orientation, not permission to speak for anyone inside the frame.

The shared word is *everyone*; the frames are not the same. One compresses persons to predict responses. The other universalizes a moral claim: "I will cease to live as self, and will take as my self my fellow-creatures"; "a lamp for them who need a lamp." Researchers and planners already run planet-scale simulations in the serving direction, and have for years. In March 2020, an individual-based epidemic simulation at Imperial College — a synthetic copy of the populations of Britain and America, every simulated person placed in a household — projected roughly 510,000 British deaths in an unmitigated epidemic and, in the report's own words, "informed policymaking in the UK and other countries" within weeks. Urban planners simulate whole metro populations — every household and person — to plan transit investments. Those welfare uses share a signature: they simulate people as *bodies* — disease vectors, commuters, households. The relevant boundary here is whether belief formation becomes an intervention surface — the line between simulating what crowds do and simulating what persons believe.

There is a well-worn genre in which a contemplative reads a frightening technical paper, reaches for an ancient text, and the ancient text wins. Naming the trap does not establish that this piece escaped it. Shantideva does not refute Light Society; nothing in the *Bodhicharyavatara* tells you whether an embedding-MLP preserves covariance structure. What the old text supplies is an orientation, not a verdict. A universal frame can orient service, but it cannot confer standing or excuse paternalism. Every deployment chooses a direction, whatever vocabulary its builders use. Which direction gets industrialized is decided by builders, buyers, regulators, and users.

---

Where does that leave the person whose cohort can be consulted without them?

The published method predicts stance changes among profile-conditioned agents; Aaru sells simulated audiences as an alternative to surveys and focus groups. The capability may improve: in Anthropic's own study, each successive generation of its Claude models was rated more persuasive than the last. So hold the object accurately, limits included. In Light Society's billion-agent run, the tabular surrogate represents three stance labels and no metacognitive state. It has no operation for inspecting its own stance transition or asking what produced it. That is a limit of this implementation, not evidence that noticing is unsimulable in principle. The consequential choices are attributable: objectives, release conditions, clients, uses, and legal constraints. Most are made by builders, firms, and governments.

Shantideva closes his book's final chapter with a vow the Dalai Lama would offer — unattributed, in another translation — at the close of his Nobel lecture twelve centuries later. In Barnett's older rendering: "As long as the heavens and the earth abide, may I continue to overcome the world's sorrows." The danger is not that the row secretly contains you. It is that an institution can consult the row instead of you, then act on what it predicts without giving you a way to inspect, contest, or correct the representation. Shantideva names an obligation, not a governance regime. A simulated public may forecast a response; it cannot authorize a decision made in the public's name.
