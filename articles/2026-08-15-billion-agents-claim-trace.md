## Domain: paper-numbers

### CONFIRMED
**Claim:** The lookup table has shape [10,000 x 10,000 x 3 x 3] = "nine hundred million cells", covering all input combinations, and during the billion-agent simulation every meeting was "a single array lookup".

**Evidence:** v2 (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12078v2): "precompute a four-dimensional lookup table T[iprof,tprof,istance,tstance]→final_stance" with "shape [10,000×10,000×3×3]" covering "all 900 million possible input combinations"; and "during simulation, each interaction reduces to a single array lookup." The draft's quoted fragment "a single array lookup" is verbatim; 10,000×10,000×3×3 = 900,000,000, so "nine hundred million cells" is arithmetically exact.

### NEEDS_RECAST
**Claim:** The billion-agent run was opinion diffusion, 100 rounds, with 200 million influencers and 800 million influencees/receivers, seeded messages propagating.

**Evidence:** v2: opinion diffusion over a Barabási–Albert scale-free network with one billion nodes; "The top 20% (200 million) serve as influencers, and the remaining 80% (800 million) as influencees"; "simulation proceeds for 100 rounds. In each round, 1% of all influencers (approximately 2 million nodes)" interact. Seeding schemes are initial STANCE distributions, not messages: "1D1N (50% disagree, 50% neutral), 1A1N (50% agree, 50% neutral), and Random." (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12078v2)

**Recast applied in v2:** All counts and the 100 rounds are confirmed, but two wording fixes: (a) replace "seeded messages propagating" with "seeded stance distributions propagating" (or "seeded opinions") — the paper seeds initial agree/neutral/disagree stances (e.g., 50% disagree / 50% neutral), not messages; (b) optionally note that in each round only 1% of influencers (~2 million) act, so "one hundred rounds of it" should not imply all billion agents interact every round.

### CONFIRMED
**Claim:** The billion agents share 10,000 distinct profiles; profiles come from a cleaned WVS Wave 7 pool of 96,125 valid entries.

**Evidence:** v2: "Each agent is assigned a demographic profile sampled from a fixed pool of 10,000 profiles drawn from the cleaned WVS dataset"; "the World Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7 dataset (2017–2022)"; "Records with incomplete responses on core fields (e.g., gender, age) were excluded, yielding a cleaned dataset of 96,125 valid entries." (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12078v2)

### NEEDS_RECAST

**Evidence:** v2: "The teacher LLM is Gemini 2.0 Flash. **Recast applied in v2:** The substance is confirmed; add the per-topic qualifier the source carries: "they had a full LLM play out roughly four hundred thousand of these opinion exchanges per topic" (paper: "For each topic, approximately 400,000 interaction tuples"). **Evidence:** arXiv 2304.03442 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442), v1 submitted Fri, 7 Apr 2023 — so "three years ago" relative to 2026-08-15 is fair rounding (3 years 4 months). Abstract: "a small town of twenty five agents" in "an interactive sandbox environment inspired by The Sims" — 25 agents confirmed. "Smallville" is NOT in the abstract but IS the paper's own name for the town, confirmed in the full text (Section 3, via https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.03442): "This sprite-based sandbox game world, Smallville, evokes a small town environment." Someone DID tell one agent to want to throw the party — the seed was user-specified. **Recast applied in v2:** Three years ago the state of this art was a video-game village: twenty-five language-model agents in a sandbox town called Smallville, waking up, cooking breakfast, spreading invitations to a Valentine's party on their own — the researchers had planted the party idea in a single agent's mind, and the other twenty-four learned of it, asked each other on dates, and showed up, none of it scripted.

## Domain: critiques

### CONFIRMED
**Claim:** their survey answers resembling Western, educated, industrialized populations and degrading as you move away from them

**Evidence:** Atari et al., "Which Humans?" (fetched from https://evolvinglanguage.ch/wp-content/uploads/Which-Humans-Atari-et-al.pdf, extracted with pdftotext). Abstract: "their performance on cognitive psychological tasks most resembles that of people from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies but declines rapidly as we move away from these populations (r = -.70)." The main analysis behind the r=-.70 is in fact survey-based, which supports the draft's "survey answers" wording: the paper uses World Values Survey Round Seven data ("World Values Survey (WVS), to offer a global comparison"), and reports: "We correlated the correspondence between average human responses and GPT responses on all variables in each of the 65 national samples... Figure 3 shows a substantial inverse correlation between cultural distance from the United States and GPT-human resemblance (r = -.70, p < .001)." Minor stylistic note only: the draft expands WEIRD as "Western, educated, industrialized," dropping "rich and democratic" — an abbreviation, not a distortion.

### CONFIRMED
**Claim:** showing less variance than real humans show

**Evidence:** Wu et al., "LLM-Based Social Simulations Require a Boundary" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19806). Abstract: "fewer than half explicitly assess behavioral variance, and most that do report lower variance than human populations." The draft's blanket-sounding phrasing is attributed to "the research record," which matches the source's systematic-review framing. The draft's compression is fair but sits at the outer edge of what the source states.

### NEEDS_RECAST
**Claim:** minor rewording of the persona prompt shifted simulated cooperation rates by up to seventy-six percentage points

**Evidence:** Ye et al. (Jinyi Ye, Lei Cao, Ding Chen, Emilio Ferrara), "Stop Drawing Scientific Claims from LLM Social Simulations Without Robustness Audits" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18890). Abstract, verbatim: "Minor perturbations in persona format and game-instruction framing shift cooperation rates by up to 76 percentage points." The 76-point figure covers perturbations in TWO design elements — persona format AND game-instruction framing — not persona prompt rewording alone. "Format" is also not necessarily "rewording" (it may be structural). The draft misattributes the whole effect to the persona prompt.

**Recast applied in v2:** minor changes to the persona format and the framing of the game instructions shifted simulated cooperation rates by up to seventy-six percentage points

### NEEDS_RECAST
**Claim:** even when simulated averages matched real survey averages, nearly half the relationships between variables came out wrong, and a third of those flipped sign

**Evidence:** Bisbee et al., "Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models," Political Analysis 32 (2024) 401–416 (open-access PDF fetched from Cambridge Core, verified via pdftotext). Verbatim: "48% of coefficients estimated from the ChatGPT responses are statistically significantly different from their ANES-derived counterpart; among these cases, the sign of the effect flips 32% of the time." The paper's own gloss: "the synthetic sample would frequently lead us to draw different inferences than if we relied on human respondents." Two overshoots in the draft: (1) "came out wrong" is stronger than "statistically significantly different from the human-data estimate" — some of the 48% could differ in magnitude while supporting the same inference; the paper commits only to "different inferences... frequently." (2) "averages matched" also overshoots: the paper says "At the coarsest level of analysis, synthetic ChatGPT opinions look remarkably similar to human ANES respondents. However, even when we compare overall average responses, we find problems with how well ChatGPT recovers the distribution of public opinion, both in terms of conditional averages and in terms of precision."

**Recast applied in v2:** even where simulated averages looked remarkably close to the real survey's, nearly half the estimated relationships between variables differed significantly from what the human data showed — and among those, the sign flipped a third of the time

### NEEDS_RECAST
**Claim:** another population-scale team showed a ten-million-agent opinion simulation could be reproduced with 381 times fewer model calls at almost no cost in accuracy

**Evidence:** Zheng et al., "APS: Bias-Controlled Adaptive Prototype Simulation for Population-Scale LLM Agents" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27419). Abstract: "APS achieves a 381.1-fold reduction over full simulation, with reference-aligned final-round JSD of 0.094," demonstrated on "a 10M-agent, multi-round public-opinion simulation," with the reduction in online LLM calls measured against "the corresponding full-LLM reference." Two problems: (1) "almost no cost in accuracy" is the draft's gloss, not the source's — a final-round Jensen–Shannon divergence of 0.094 is a small but real, measured divergence (roughly 13% of the JSD maximum if natural-log-bounded), and the paper does not characterize it as negligible. (2) The reference is the full-LLM run, not human data — the draft's "reproduced" is compatible with this, but "accuracy" invites a misreading as accuracy against reality; the fidelity claim is only to the full simulation's own output.

**Recast applied in v2:** another population-scale team showed a ten-million-agent opinion simulation could be approximately reproduced with 381 times fewer model calls — the cheap run's final opinion distribution diverging only slightly (JSD 0.094) from the full run's

### CONFIRMED
**Claim:** Past a certain size, crowds of copies converge to smooth curves

**Evidence:** Flint, Aiello, Pastor-Satorras, Baronchelli, "Group size effects and collective misalignment in LLM multi-agent systems" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22422). Abstract, verbatim: "we develop a mean-field analytical approach and show that, above a critical population size, simulations converge to deterministic predictions that expose the basins of attraction of competing equilibria." "Smooth curves" is a fair colloquial rendering of convergence to deterministic mean-field predictions, and "crowds of copies" matches the paper's setup (populations of a given LLM in a coordination game; the abstract speaks of "model-dependent dynamical regimes," i.e., homogeneous populations per model). Two bounds the editor should hold: the abstract does not literally say "copies," and the finding is demonstrated in one coordination-game setting, so the draft's use of it as a general law of large simulations is an induction from this result, not a direct quote of it. As worded — a short general gloss cited to this paper — it is within fair-summary range.

### NEEDS_RECAST
**Claim:** In a preregistered trial published in Nature Human Behaviour, GPT-4 given a debate partner's basic personal information raised its odds of moving that person's view by 81.7 percent over human persuaders

**Evidence:** The 81.7% figure is the arXiv preprint number: arXiv:2403.14380 abstract says "participants who debated GPT-4 with access to their personal information had 81.7% (p < 0.01; N=820 unique participants) higher odds of increased agreement with their opponents compared to participants who debated humans." But the PUBLISHED version — "On the conversational persuasiveness of GPT-4," Nature Human Behaviour (2025), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02194-6 — reports a different number: "81.2% relative increase in odds of higher post-debate agreement" (95% CI [+26.0%, +160.7%], P < 0.01), and "GPT-4 with personalization was more persuasive 64.4% of the time." The draft attributes the preprint's 81.7% to the Nature publication — the exact number-mixing the trace law forbids.

**Recast applied in v2:** In a preregistered trial published in Nature Human Behaviour, GPT-4 given a debate partner's basic personal information raised its odds of moving that person's view by 81.2 percent over human persuaders. (If the 81.7% figure is kept, it must be attributed to the 2024 preprint, not the Nature publication.)
