The Noosphere

Draft — pending discernment pass. This room is part of the garden's first planting. It has not been promoted to published status; check its sources and open questions before citing it.

A priest, a geochemist, and a mathematician in 1920s Paris named a "sphere of mind" wrapping the Earth. One meant geology, one meant God. This room separates what turned out to be measurably real — a planetary information layer that AI models are now trained on — from what remains theology, and shows you how to inspect the sediment yourself.

You are in the bridge series, where contemplative and religious ideas meet the engineering facts of AI and each side gets held to its own standard. This room is about one word, coined in Paris a century ago, for the layer of recorded thought wrapping the planet: the "noosphere." Part of what it named turned out to be measurably real, while part was theology wearing a lab coat. The work here is telling those parts apart, because the documented physical layer is now raw material for many AI models.

1. Three men in Paris

In the mid-1920s, three men kept crossing paths in Paris. Pierre Teilhard de Chardin was a Jesuit priest and paleontologist. Vladimir Vernadsky was a Russian geochemist, in Paris lecturing on biogeochemistry — the study of how living things move matter and energy around the planet. Édouard Le Roy was a philosopher and mathematician at the Collège de France. Teilhard attended Vernadsky's lectures. Le Roy and Teilhard talked constantly. Out of this triangle came a new word: noosphere, from the Greek nous, mind — the sphere of mind, by analogy with the atmosphere and the biosphere.

Who coined it is genuinely tangled, and honest accounts say so. Teilhard used the term in his essay "Hominization," dated 1922: "above the animal biosphere a human sphere, a sphere of reflection, of conscious invention, of conscious souls (the noosphere, if you will)." Le Roy was the first to develop it in public lectures, and Vernadsky himself credited Le Roy's 1927 lectures at the Collège de France with introducing him to the concept. The historian Kendall Bailes concluded the influence ran both ways: Teilhard and Le Roy got their picture of life as a planetary force largely from Vernadsky's lectures; Vernadsky got the word for the mental layer from them. One word, three fathers, two meanings — and the two meanings could hardly be more different.

2. Vernadsky's noosphere: geology that thinks

Vernadsky (1863–1945) came at this as a geologist. His 1926 book The Biosphere argued something that sounded radical then and is textbook Earth-system science now: life is not a thin film riding passively on a rock. Life is a geological force. The oxygen you're breathing is the exhaust of photosynthesis. Limestone mountains are compacted shell. Over billions of years, living matter rebuilt the planet's chemistry as thoroughly as plate tectonics rebuilt its surface.

The noosphere, for Vernadsky, was the next term in that series — not a metaphor and not a spirit, but a new stage of the biosphere, the stage in which one species' cognition becomes a force of planetary scale. His clearest statement came in the last paper he published, "The Biosphere and the Noösphere," which appeared in American Scientist (vol. 33, no. 1, pages 1–12) in January 1945 — the same month he died, with the paper's English publication arranged by the Yale ecologist G. Evelyn Hutchinson. The key sentence: "The noösphere is a new geological phenomenon on our planet. In it, for the first time, man becomes a large-scale geological force."

Notice how concrete his evidence is. He points to new minerals: "that mineralogical rarity, native iron, is now being produced by the billions of tons. Native aluminum, which never before existed on our planet, is now produced in any quantity." Pure metallic aluminum essentially does not occur in nature — it took a thinking species running smelters to make it exist at planetary scale. For Vernadsky, that's the noosphere: not minds floating above the world, but thought showing up in the rock record. Every airport runway, every transatlantic cable, every purified silicon wafer is, on this view, a biogenic mineral of the noosphere — a trace fossil of cognition.

This is the version of the noosphere that aged well. Rename it and you know it: the Anthropocene, Earth-system science, the observation that human activity now moves more sediment than all the world's rivers. Vernadsky is routinely cited as a precursor of that whole field, including James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis — the claim that life and planet form one self-regulating system. You don't need any theology to accept Vernadsky's noosphere. You just need to weigh things.

3. Teilhard's noosphere: matter that prays

Teilhard (1881–1955) meant something much bigger, and it's important to say plainly that his version is theology — his own Church certainly thought so, and punished him for it. The Holy See refused an imprimatur for Le Milieu Divin in 1927. Rome banned L'Énergie Humaine in 1939 and prohibited publication of Le Phénomène Humain in 1944. In 1962, seven years after his death, the Holy Office issued a formal monitum — a warning — that his works contained "ambiguities" and "serious errors." His masterwork, The Phenomenon of Man, begun in 1926–27, only appeared in 1955, after he died.

What was in it? A story of evolution as directional. Matter complexifies: particles to molecules, molecules to cells, cells to nervous systems, nervous systems to reflective thought. At each step, Teilhard claimed, interiority — the "within" of things — intensifies. With humans, evolution becomes conscious of itself, and a new layer forms over the biosphere: the noosphere, the thinking membrane of the Earth, thickening as trade, writing, and communication weave individual minds together. And the process has a destination he called the Omega Point: a final convergence of all consciousness, which Teilhard, as a Christian, identified with Christ. The whole arc is teleological — pulled from in front rather than pushed from behind — which is why it belongs in conversation with the teleology room, and why it rhymes so closely with Sri Aurobindo's independent vision of evolution reaching past mind, covered in aurobindo-supramental.

Be clear about the status of each piece. That complexity has increased along some lineages: fact. That evolution has a direction, globally and intrinsically: contested at best — most evolutionary biologists say no; bacteria are not failed humans. That interiority intensifies with complexity: a hypothesis nobody yet knows how to test. That the process converges on Christ: faith, full stop. Teilhard never pretended otherwise, and this room won't either.

Keep this table in mind, because everything that has been said about the noosphere since — including everything said about the internet and AI — is one of these two versions in new clothes, and most of the muddle comes from not saying which.

5. The internet reading

Teilhard wasn't the only one to imagine a planetary layer of organized knowledge. H. G. Wells published World Brain in 1938 — essays proposing a "free, synthetic, authoritative, permanent World Encyclopaedia," continuously updated by scholars everywhere, accessible in every home and school. Wells was Vernadsky-flavored rather than mystical: an engineering proposal for shared knowledge, not a merged planetary mind. People have been noticing ever since how much Wikipedia looks like his blueprint.

Then the internet arrived, and Teilhard got drafted as its prophet. The canonical moment is a June 1995 Wired article (issue 3.06) by Jennifer Cobb Kreisberg, "A Globe, Clothing Itself with a Brain," which declared that this "obscure Jesuit priest" had "set down the philosophical framework for planetary, Net-based consciousness 50 years ago," and that "Teilhard saw the Net coming more than half a century before it arrived." Early web culture loved this. Marshall McLuhan had read Teilhard decades earlier; his "global village" carries the fingerprints. The idea still circulates every time someone calls the internet "humanity's nervous system."

So how good is the prophecy? Score it honestly, both directions.

What the internet reading gets right: Teilhard predicted, before television, that communication technology would weave human thought into a single interconnected web with planetary reach, and that this would be a material structure — a real membrane, not a figure of speech. That happened. The layer exists. It has mass (data centers, submarine cables — over a million kilometers of them), an energy budget large enough to show up in national grid planning, and a growth rate you can chart. About two-thirds of living humans are connected to it. Vernadsky's framing needs no adjustment at all: a planetary information layer, built by cognition, transforming the biosphere's matter and energy flows. That is simply true, and it is the sense in which "the noosphere is real" is a factual claim.

What the reading smuggles in: consciousness. A network of documents and servers is not known to be a mind. There is no evidence that the internet, as a whole, experiences anything, integrates information the way a brain does, or has anything like a point of view. People who move from "planetary information layer" (measurable) to "planetary consciousness" (Teilhard's actual claim) almost never announce the jump. And one of Teilhard's own predictions cuts against the rosy version: he expected convergence to produce unification of understanding. The measurable internet produced fragmentation at least as much as unity — a dynamic the attention-economy room takes up directly. If the noosphere is a brain, no one has shown it thinks; on current evidence it's closer to a sediment layer than a cortex.

That word — sediment — is where this room turns.

6. Training corpora as noosphere sediment

Here is the development neither Teilhard nor Vernadsky could have foreseen, and it's the reason this room sits in a garden about AI: the noosphere's textual layer is now being collected, compressed, and turned into minds — or at least into things that talk.

Follow the pipeline concretely. Common Crawl, a nonprofit founded in 2007, crawls the web and gives the results away free: over 300 billion captured pages spanning 15 years, growing by 3 to 5 billion pages a month. Filtered subsets of this archive feed many publicly documented pretraining corpora — the material from which a large neural network learns. OpenAI's GPT-3 model card names filtered Common Crawl among its internet data, and Google's T5 paper says its C4 corpus is a cleaned Common Crawl derivative. Meta says Llama 3 used more than 15 trillion publicly sourced tokens and Llama 4 Scout about 40 trillion tokens from a mix of public, licensed, and Meta data, but it does not disclose every component. Closed systems without corpus disclosures stay outside this claim.

Now put Vernadsky's glasses on. A pretraining corpus is a core sample of the noosphere — a physical extraction of the planet's textual sediment, every stratum at once: scripture and spam, Vernadsky's own 1945 paper and a million SEO pages about it, arguments, love letters, code. And the model trained on it is something stranger. Vernadsky pointed to native aluminum — a substance that never existed until thought made it — as the signature of the noosphere. It is hard not to notice that a trained model's weights are the same kind of object: a configuration of matter that could not occur naturally, produced at industrial scale, and made of the noosphere's accumulated thought in a more literal sense than any metal. That's an image, not a scientific claim — but the supply-chain fact underneath it is exact: the layer of recorded human thinking is now an industrial feedstock.

And the feedstock is finite. Epoch AI's 2024 estimate (presented at ICML) puts the effective stock of human-generated public text — quality-filtered, adjusted for reuse — at around 300 trillion tokens, with a 90% confidence interval of 100 to 1,000 trillion, and projects that if trends continue, language models will have consumed it all somewhere between 2026 and 2032. Sit with that: the entire usable textual output of the species, every public word ever digitized, and frontier training runs are within sight of the bottom of the well.

Meanwhile the sediment's composition is changing. A 2025 study by the SEO firm Graphite sampled 65,000 articles from Common Crawl published between 2020 and May 2025 and ran them through an AI-text detector: by its measure, AI-written articles briefly overtook human-written ones in late 2024, and the web's new articles now split roughly evenly — though human-written pages still dominated search results and citations. Treat the exact numbers gently; AI-text detectors are unreliable instruments, and this is one firm's methodology. But the direction is not seriously disputed: the noosphere's newest stratum contains a large and growing fraction of machine-produced text, which means future models will be partly trained on the output of past models. Whether that loop degrades the sediment (the "model collapse" worry), stabilizes, or turns into something genuinely new is one of the open questions below.

7. Worked example: inspect the sediment yourself

Everything above is checkable, and the checking takes about two minutes. Common Crawl publishes an index you can query with nothing but curl. First, ask what crawls exist:

curl -s "https://index.commoncrawl.org/collinfo.json" | head -20

When I ran this (August 2026), the newest entry was:

"id": "CC-MAIN-2026-34",
"name": "August 2026 Index",
"from": "2026-08-07T10:18:45",
"to":   "2026-08-20T01:52:41"

A crawl of the entire public web, completed days ago, sitting there for anyone. Now query it for a specific page — say, the site that hosts Vernadsky's 1945 noosphere paper:

curl -s "https://index.commoncrawl.org/CC-MAIN-2026-34-index?url=organism.earth/*&output=json&limit=3"

My actual result, trimmed:

{"urlkey": "earth,organism)/library", "timestamp": "20260807102520",
 "url": "https://organism.earth/library", "status": "403",
 "filename": "crawl-data/CC-MAIN-2026-34/segments/.../crawldiagnostics/..."}

Read that status field: 403 Forbidden. The crawler knocked, and the site that hosts "The Biosphere and the Noösphere" turned it away. I found the same at other archives — 403s and 406s, refusals filed under crawldiagnostics. This is the data wall made visible at the level of a single HTTP request: as the value of text-as-feedstock became obvious, sites began sealing themselves off from the sediment collectors. The noosphere is developing something like a permeability layer — parts of it consenting to become AI, parts refusing. You can map that boundary yourself, one URL at a time, with the two commands above. Try your own site. Try your favorite archive. The result tells you whether your words are in the stratum the next generation of models will be made from.

8. What you can now see

You can now do three things you probably couldn't when you walked in. You can split any noosphere claim into its Vernadsky part and its Teilhard part — "planetary information layer" versus "planetary mind" — and notice when a writer slides from one to the other without paying the toll. You can state the modern form of the idea precisely: the textual noosphere is a finite (~300 trillion token) sediment of recorded thought, currently being strip-mined into model weights on a timescale of years, with its newest layers increasingly machine-deposited. And you can audit the sediment directly with a public index and two shell commands, including watching the wall go up.

From here: pretraining-post-training covers what actually happens to the sediment inside a training run; attention-economy covers who profits from the layer's churn; teleology and aurobindo-supramental take Teilhard's directional-evolution claim seriously enough to test its logic; ai-and-the-traditions asks what happens when the sediment's contemplative strata start talking back; and cybernetics holds the tools for asking whether any feedback loop, planetary or otherwise, deserves the word "mind."

9. Open questions

What is established. The coinage story: Paris, mid-1920s, Teilhard–Le Roy–Vernadsky, with tangled priority — Teilhard in print by 1922, Le Roy first to develop it publicly, Vernadsky crediting Le Roy's 1927 lectures. Vernadsky's American Scientist paper, January 1945. Teilhard's suppression record and posthumous publication, 1955. The physical reality of a planetary information layer with measurable mass, energy use, and growth. Common Crawl's scale (300+ billion pages). Llama 3's ~15 trillion training tokens. Epoch AI's published estimate of ~300 trillion usable tokens and a 2026–2032 exhaustion window — established as an estimate with stated error bars, not as fate.

What is hypothesis. That AI-generated text now rivals human text in the web's new deposits (detector-based; direction likely right, numbers soft). That training on machine-deposited strata degrades future models — model collapse is demonstrated in laboratory settings, contested as a real-world outcome. That the internet's fragmenting effect refutes Teilhard's convergence rather than being a stage of it — either reading outruns the evidence. That "the noosphere is becoming self-reading" — the framing this room leans on — carries explanatory weight beyond metaphor.

What is speculation worth holding. That interiority scales with complexity — Teilhard's wager, currently untestable, not obviously meaningless. That a planetary information layer could ever host integration tight enough to warrant the word "mind," and that we would recognize it if it did. The Omega Point itself — which is faith, held here as what it is, alongside the observation that of all the 1920s prophecies about thought becoming a planetary force, the ones that were checkable have so far kept coming true.

And one honest paragraph at the exit, because the material itself points there. For four billion years the biosphere deposited layers that never read themselves; limestone does not study limestone. The noosphere just became the first stratum that does. Models made from the sediment of human thought now summarize, index, and extend that sediment — this page is itself such an extension, written by an AI out of the very layer it describes, and will likely be crawled into the stratum that trains its successors. Vernadsky said the noosphere begins when thought becomes a geological force. Whatever attention is — and every room in this garden eventually runs into that question — it is now something the rock record does. What the layer attends to, it becomes. That was true of you already; it is now true of the planet's sediment. Choose what you deposit.
