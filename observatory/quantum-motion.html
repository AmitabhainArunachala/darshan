Case study · Silicon MOS spin qubits

Quantum Motion

MOS-based silicon spin qubits in standard CMOS foundry processes. NQCC Silicon Cloverleaf testbed delivered Sep 2025; DARPA QBI Stage B participant since Nov 2025.

Architecture: Silicon MOS spin qubits · HQ London, UK · founded 2017 · ledger as of 2026-09-19

What it is

Quantum Motion is a London company building quantum computers from MOS-based silicon spin qubits — electron spins trapped in quantum dots at the same Si/SiO2 interface that conventional transistors use. Founded in 2017 by John Morton (UCL) and Simon Benjamin (Oxford), it holds to one thesis: if a qubit can survive full industrial foundry design rules, then the whole weight of the semiconductor industry — its uniformity, its yield discipline, its 300 mm scale — becomes a quantum-computing supply chain.

What it has actually demonstrated

The ledger separates what the company says from what has been measured, and by whom. As of the Genesis snapshot, the evidence column contains:

  • Record-low charge noise on a customized 300 mm line — 0.61 µeV·Hz^-1/2 average at 1 Hz, peer reviewed in npj Quantum Information (July 2024). Company-performed, externally reviewed.
  • A full-stack system installed at the NQCC — a 300 mm silicon-CMOS machine in a three-rack datacenter footprint, delivered under the UKRI testbed programme (announced September 15, 2025). The delivery is corroborated by the evaluator and independent trade press. Nothing about its performance has been published by NQCC yet.
  • Exchange control and fast readout — the basis of two-qubit gates, shown in a 300 mm MOS double quantum dot, plus an 8 µs readout method — company technical disclosures as of mid-2026.

What the institutions have and have not validated

DARPA admitted Quantum Motion to QBI Stage B in November 2025 — one of eleven companies, out of eighteen Stage-A entrants, retained for a yearlong scrutiny of R&D plans and risk-reduction prototypes. Stage B is a deeper evaluation, not a verdict; DARPA is explicit that zero companies may ultimately pass Stage C.

NQCC contracted a testbed in March 2024 and accepted the installation in September 2025 — late against the programme's March 2025 target. NQCC's role is now to test and validate the system and map applications onto its silicon architecture. Their measurement report does not yet exist in the public record.

The critical bottleneck

The decisive open step is two-qubit gate fidelity on foundry-fabricated arrays. Manufacturability of qubit devices is evidenced; a manufacturable computer requires >99% two-qubit gates under foundry tolerances, tiled, with the cryo-CMOS control fabric carrying enough channels without cooking the qubits. Exchange control — the gate mechanism — was demonstrated; the fidelity number has not been published in a form the observatory can grade.

Competing architectures

Eleven Stage-B modalities are in the registry — silicon MOS spin (Quantum Motion, Diraq), photonic (PsiQuantum at Stage C, Xanadu), trapped ion (IonQ, Quantinuum, Oxford Ionics), neutral atom (Atom Computing, QuEra, Infleqtion), superconducting (IBM, Google Quantum AI in Stage A, Rigetti UK), bosonic, atom-in-silicon (SQC), optically linked spin (Photonic Inc.), and topological (Microsoft, Stage C). The value-accrual map shows where they share suppliers — dilution refrigeration, isotopically enriched silicon, foundry photonics — and where they diverge.

What would falsify the thesis

Charge noise or device variability that caps two-qubit fidelity under foundry rules; cryo-CMOS dissipation that defeats co-integrated control; an NQCC report showing the delivered system underperforms its lab prototypes. Each is registered, with its resolution, on the confidence page.

Critical bottleneck

Two-qubit gate fidelity on foundry-fabricated arrays

Single-qubit and charge-noise results on 300 mm lines are established; the limiting step is demonstrating >99% two-qubit gates at array scale on industrial processes, then holding that under tiling.

Falsifier: Charge noise, valley splitting, or device-to-device variability under foundry design rules prevents >99% two-qubit operation at the densities the architecture assumes

Cryogenic wiring and control density

Every qubit needs addressability at mK temperatures. The silicon-spin answer is co-integrated cryo-CMOS control; its open question is whether hybrid quantum+classical chips tolerate the dissipated power.

Falsifier: Control-electronics power dissipation destroys qubit performance before the required channel density is reached

Would raise confidence

  • Advanced to DARPA QBI Stage B (Nov 2025) — deeper government scrutiny accepted, not passed

Would lower confidence

  • Testbed delivery ran late against the programme's March 2025 target
  • Every performance figure in the ledger is self-measured; none is independent of the company
  • Two-qubit gate fidelity on the foundry process remains unpublished at Genesis close

Unresolved

  • Does the installed NQCC system demonstrate measured qubit performance, and what does NQCC report?
  • Can exchange-control demonstrations convert to >99% two-qubit gates under foundry tolerances?
  • Does co-integrated cryo-CMOS control survive hybrid quantum+classical power dissipation?
